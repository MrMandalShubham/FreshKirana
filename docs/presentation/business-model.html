03 · Customer & problem lens

Today the system is a person's memory

B2B grocery distribution in India mostly runs the way it has for decades — and it works, right up until it has to grow.

Orders

Arrive by phone call and WhatsApp message, written into a notebook or remembered. No two channels agree on what was ordered.

Prices

Live in the owner's head. Every customer negotiates; nobody can say what margin any given order actually earned.

Credit

Tracked in a ledger book. Who owes what, since when, against which bill — answerable only by the one person who keeps it.

Stock

Known by walking the aisle. Branches over-order to be safe, or run out; nobody sees wastage until the money is already gone.

Why this breaks at scale: every branch multiplies the guesswork. N branches means N notebooks, N stock counts done by eye, N credit books — and one founder trying to hold all of it. The founder is the system, and a person doesn't scale.

The product replaces founder-memory with a control tower: one catalog, one price policy, one credit book, one view of stock across every location — while keeping the ways of working customers already trust (WhatsApp, a phone call, the counter).

04 · Investor lens

Buy. Own. Sell. Revenue is margin.

FreshKirana is not a marketplace. It buys goods at the hub, owns the stock, and sells to business customers. Revenue is the gap between what a crate costs landed and what it sells for.

Marketplace (what we pivoted away from)Distribution (what this is)
Who owns stockThird-party vendorsWe do
RevenueCommission on other people's salesGross margin: sell price − landed cost
Who owes usNobodyCustomers, on credit terms
We oweVendors (payouts)Suppliers (purchases)
Core riskVendor churnWorking capital · credit default · wastage
Core leversTake rate, basket sizeBuy price · drop size · wastage · mix · collection speed · route density

Landed cost is the number everything stands on. Purchase price + freight + loading + grading losses − supplier schemes actually realised. Get it wrong and every margin figure in the business is fiction. That's why goods-receipt is built before any margin reporting — margin truth is created at the warehouse door, not in a spreadsheet.

05 · Sales & customer lens

Three customers, three different games

Flattening these into one "B2B customer" is the classic mistake. They order differently, pay differently, and punish failure differently.

Retail shops

₹8,000–25,000 per order · weekly rhythm · the same 30–80 lines every time.

What they need: fill rate and credit. The order is a reorder — last week's list, adjusted, submitted in 90 seconds. Extremely price-aware; loyal while supply is reliable.

Failure mode: short-ship them twice and they call your competitor.

Caterers

₹15,000–60,000 per order · spikes around functions · bigger quantities per line.

What they need: date certainty. The biryani is on a wedding date; the rice is not negotiable. Values reliability over the last rupee.

Failure mode: miss one function date.

Event managers

₹50,000–3,00,000 per order · project-shaped · needs a quote first, then an exact delivery.

What they need: a quotation flow — enquiry → priced quote → acceptance → firm order with stock reserved against it, protected from everyday sales.

Best margin of the three. One miss ends the relationship — treated as a binary metric, not a percentage.

The product consequence: retail shops get an order pad, not a shopping app. Search barely matters; the pre-filled last order is the interface. Events get quotes and firm stock allocation. Caterers get promised dates that the system refuses to overbook. Order values are demo figures (D-B2) — the segmentation is the point.

06 · Operations & buying lens

Three product lines with different physics

Staples, fresh produce, and beauty are not three categories in one catalog — they obey different laws, and the system measures each by its own rules. (Margins and shares below are industry-typical demo values.)

Staples & groceryFruits & vegetablesBeauty & personal care
Gross margin3–8%12–25%12–25%
Wastage~0.5%8–15%~1%
Price changesWeekly-ishDaily — sometimes twiceStable, MRP-anchored
Stock turns / year12–2060–1204–8
The one controlBuy priceWastage + daily repricingDon't overstock

What the system does about it

  • F&V is priced cost-plus from this morning's landed cost — a weekly price list is wrong by Wednesday.
  • Wastage is posted daily, per branch, per category — never a plug figure discovered at month end.
  • Margin is reported by line, never blended. A blended 10% hides staples at 4% and beauty at 20%.

The two traps, named: F&V wastage is the single biggest margin leak in the business — a quarter of revenue rides on measuring it daily. And beauty is the working-capital trap: best margin, slowest turns. Overstocking beauty is the classic way a profitable distributor runs out of cash.

07 · Investor lens

Why this is worth building

The same platform, pointed at consumers, needed ~725 orders a day to matter. Pointed at businesses, it needs ~38.

Roughly the same contribution percentage — 3.7% against 3.8% — but the order is 20× larger and the delivery cost isn't. Delivery cost is per drop, not per rupee, so bigger orders are structurally more profitable.

The market context

Indian grocery is overwhelmingly served by the unorganised trade — lakhs of small shops, each buying from distributors weekly. B2B supply to that trade is enormous, fragmented, and barely digitised. This model doesn't need to win a city; it needs to win its own trade area, then repeat.

The replication path

Everything is designed as a playbook: a branch is a configuration, not a project. Once the gates pass at one branch — stock accurate, cash cycle healthy — the next branch is the same software with a new address.

The seam held open

B2C is deferred, not deleted (decision D-B7). Customer type is modelled from day one, so adding consumers later is configuration, not a rewrite. The B2C storefront already exists — built and live from the earlier phase of this project.

The constraint nobody puts on slide one: working capital

Distribution consumes cash as it grows. You pay suppliers before customers pay you, and stock sits in between. Here is one crate of goods, followed across fifty days:

₹18.0Llocked in stock — 20 days of inventory at cost
₹30.0Llocked in receivables — 30 days of customer credit
−₹13.5Lfunded by supplier credit — 15 days
₹34.5Lnet cash that must sit still to run ₹30L/month

The rule this imposes: the cash conversion cycle — days of stock + days of receivables − days of supplier credit — sits on the daily dashboard above revenue. No growth push is approved without stating the cash it consumes. A distributor that grows 40% on credit without funding it runs out of money while profitable — the most common death in this industry, and it looks like success until the day it doesn't.

09 · Strategy lens

Six levers, ranked — and we hold all six

A marketplace has two levers. A distributor who owns the buying has six, and the ranking below is what set the build order.

#LeverWhy it mattersWhere it lives in the product
1Buy priceAt 10% margin, buying 1% better lifts contribution ~10%. Nothing else comes close.Supplier rate comparison · hub volume consolidation · scheme tracking
2Drop sizeDelivery is paid per drop. A ₹12k order growing to ₹18k adds ~₹550 contribution.Order pad upsell · minimum order rules · standing orders
3WastageF&V wastage cut from 12% to 8% ≈ +₹120 on every order in the book.Daily wastage posting · FEFO picking (already built) · demand-led replenishment
4MixShifting 5 points of revenue from staples to beauty/F&V lifts blended margin ~0.6pt.Assortment · rep incentives · order-pad suggestions

Levers 1 and 3 are the ones a marketplace never had — and the two that most reward software, because both are invisible without measurement. You cannot negotiate what you don't track, and you cannot cut wastage you discover at year end.

10 · Operations lens

The operating model, end to end

Seven stages, from mandi morning to money in the bank. Each one names what the system does — and what's already built.

Procure

Supplier master, purchase orders, rate comparison — plus a "buy first, record now" path for mandi purchases, which never wait for paperwork. Schemes and rebates accrue into cost, not into a year-end surprise.

New

Receive

Goods receipt at the door: count, check, record shorts and damage at receipt, capture batch and expiry, apportion freight — and freeze the landed cost. The most important new module: this is where margin truth is created.

New

Distribute

Reorder points per branch per product, computed from real sell-through. Transfer suggestions generated centrally, moved on a delivery challan (same state, one GSTIN — no tax leg), tracked in transit, received at the branch.

New

Sell

Order pad pre-filled from the last order · customer-specific price list · credit check as a hard gate before confirmation · firm stock allocation for events · quotes that convert to orders. Partial fulfilment is a normal outcome, not a failure.

Re-pointed

Fulfil

FEFO picking lists, batch selection, weighing of loose goods in grams, invoice generated on actual quantities shipped. Largely built and tested — the grocery-mechanics phases did this work already.

Built

Deliver

Delivery days and route beats, not 2-hour slots. Own and hired vehicles, both costed per trip. Driver collects payment at the door, picks up returns and crates on the same trip. Proof of delivery captured.

New

Collect

Every invoice starts an ageing clock. Receipts apply against specific invoices, never a floating balance. Credit notes for returns and rate differences. Overdue accounts climb an escalation ladder that ends in a supply stop.

11 · Sales lens

Every channel, one order

Be honest about how B2B orders actually arrive: by WhatsApp and by phone, to a person. The system meets customers where they are — and writes every channel to the same order object.

WhatsApp

Most shops and caterers order this way today. The WhatsApp infrastructure — templates, inbound webhook, quick replies — is already built from the earlier phase and gets rewired to B2B flows.

The order pad

Last order pre-filled, change quantities, running total, live credit balance, submit in 90 seconds. The margin lever: costs nothing per order and grows drop size. Built on the "usual basket" engine that's already live.

Telesales & field reps

A rep places the order for the customer — on a call or standing at their counter — attributed correctly, priced from the customer's own list.

Counter walk-in

Branches sell over the counter (decision D-B3). Recorded from day one — because every counter sale moves stock — with a full point-of-sale experience deferred.

The credit gate — where sales meets discipline

Before any order confirms: available credit = limit − outstanding − undelivered confirmed orders. Within limit and current → confirm. Overdue → escalating approvals. Over limit or 30+ days overdue → blocked, cash-only. Every override is logged: who, when, why, how much. A credit gate everyone can click past is worse than none.

One rule keeps this sane: the channel is an attribute on the order, never a separate code path. Four intake channels, one order pipeline, one truth to reconcile.

12 · Operations & inventory lens

Stock truth, at every location

Allocation, replenishment and valuation all stand on one thing: the system's stock number matching the shelf. Everything on this page defends that match.

  • Per-branch stock with four states: on hand, reserved by in-flight orders, firm-allocated to confirmed events, and in transit between locations. In-transit stock is sellable from nowhere.
  • The oversell guard is already built — an atomic reservation check, proven under 20 racing checkouts, carried over from the earlier build. Ported, not rewritten.
  • Batches and expiry are already built — every lot a row, first-expiry-first-out picking, shelf-life sweeps, and a recall that produces a regulator-ready report.
  • Weighted-average cost valuation — every unit carries its landed cost, so stock value and cost of goods fall out of the ledger, not a spreadsheet.
  • Replenishment is computed, not guessed: reorder point = average daily sell-through × lead time + safety stock, per branch, per product, on a rolling window.
  • Wastage posted daily — damage, expiry, shrinkage — per branch, per category. Cycle counts reconcile shelf to system, and every variance posts a ledger entry that explains itself.

The quiet killer: unrecorded counter sales. A walk-in buying 5 kg of atta removes stock the system still believes is there. Unrecorded, the error compounds daily and silently breaks allocation, replenishment and valuation at once.

So counter sales are recorded from day one — a small entry that depletes stock and posts revenue. The stock-truth half ships now; the point-of-sale experience half is deferred.

13 · Finance lens

A ledger that cannot lie

Every rupee that moves posts a balanced double-entry transaction — refused in code, refused by the service, and refused a third time by the database itself. Margin, receivables and cash positions are computed from the ledger, never summed from order rows.

One credit sale, followed through (₹12,000 sold, ₹10,800 landed cost, 5% GST):

— sale on credit — Dr customer_receivable(shop) 12,600 Cr sales_revenue 12,000 Cr gst_output_payable 600 Dr cost_of_goods 10,800 Cr inventory(branch) 10,800 ← margin ₹1,200 falls straight out — driver collects at the door — Dr cash_in_transit(driver) 12,600 Cr customer_receivable(shop) 12,600 — driver banks it — Dr bank 12,600 Cr cash_in_transit(driver) 12,600

Two controls fall out for free: a non-zero customer_receivable past its due date is the overdue amount, and a non-zero cash_in_transit after the deposit deadline is the driver's cash shortfall. Both surface automatically. No reconciliation spreadsheet exists anywhere in this business.

14 · Engineering lens

Built on a foundation that already ships

This is not a greenfield promise. The platform is live on Google Cloud with 753 automated tests, continuous deployment, and a year of grocery-specific mechanics already proven.

Stack

TypeScript end to end · NestJS API · PostgreSQL + PostGIS · Redis · Drizzle ORM · Next.js PWA storefront · Google Cloud Run, Mumbai region · CI deploys both services on every green merge.

Architecture

A modular monolith: one deployable, 22 bounded modules, each owning its own database schema. Boundaries are enforced by CI — a module reaching into another's internals fails the build. Services extract later on published triggers, not by fashion.

Discipline that carries over

Idempotency keys on every mutating endpoint · transactional outbox for events · analytics events shipped with every feature · double-entry invariant asserted in tests, in the service, and in the database itself.
