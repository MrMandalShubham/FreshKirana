The problem

Indian households shop for groceries in two different ways. Only one of them is served.

Today the planned shop means walking to the kirana, or sending a WhatsApp list to a shop that calls back about missing items, negotiates substitutions out loud, and hand-writes the bill. It works. It is also slow, error-prone, and has no prices, no history, and no way to reorder.

Quick commerce serves this badly on purpose. A dark store carries 2,000–6,000 items chosen for impulse buying. A planned basket needs the long tail — staples, regional brands, loose produce — that no dark store stocks.

What we are building

The kirana already has the range. It is missing everything around it.

We do not build warehouses or hold stock. We connect three people who are already in the neighbourhood, and give each of them the one thing they are missing.

The whole product in one line. The shop never leaves WhatsApp; the shopper never chases the shop.

Why we win

Three advantages a competitor cannot copy quickly.

The shop stays on WhatsApp

A kirana owner will not learn a dashboard. They already live in WhatsApp, so that is where the entire vendor product lives — accepting orders, flagging what is missing, confirming handover.

Dashboard-first rivals must rebuild

Your basket predicts itself

Groceries are the one category where history genuinely predicts the next order: 70–80% of a household’s items repeat. A 40-item monthly list becomes one tap.

Compounds with every order

Built for messy stock

Small shops will never have perfect inventory data. We assume that and design around it — confirm before charging, substitute generously, promise slots conservatively.

Rivals assuming clean data break on contact
How the money works

Every order carries its own weight.

The model below is our planning assumption, to be checked against a real pilot. It is deliberately conservative: no advertising revenue, no private label, no subscription.

Per orderAmount
Average basket₹600
Commission from the shop (~10%)+₹60
Delivery and packaging fees+₹30
Delivery cost−₹40
Packaging, payments, support−₹14
Refunds, shrinkage, failed cash deliveries−₹13
Contribution per order≈ ₹23

Lever one — basket size

Delivery costs the same whether the basket is ₹600 or ₹900. A larger basket roughly doubles contribution. This is exactly what the repeat-basket feature is for.

Lever two — two orders, one trip

Because customers choose a delivery slot rather than demanding ten minutes, we can batch. Two orders on one trip cuts ₹40 to about ₹24.

Both levers are product decisions, not operational grind. That is the whole reason we chose slots over speed.

How it is put together

One system, four front doors.

Everything runs on Google Cloud and costs almost nothing when idle. The important design choice is that all four audiences talk to the same core — so a price, a stock level, or an order status can never disagree between them.

Built so that pulling any one audience out later — a bigger vendor app, a partner integration — does not mean rebuilding the middle.

Straight answers

What is real today, and what is not.

Every number above is from a working system. Here is the honest line between what has been built and what has not, so nothing comes as a surprise later.

Real and working

  • A shopper can browse, order, pay and track — on a phone
  • A shop can run an order start to finish from WhatsApp
  • Stock is held at checkout, so two people cannot buy the last packet
  • Cash orders are risk-scored and confirmed before a shop packs them
  • Refunds are issued automatically when an order is cancelled or an item is missing
  • Loose produce is weighed and the customer is charged for what was delivered
  • An unsafe batch can be pulled and its customers identified

Not built yet

  • No pilot has run. No real customer, shop or rupee yet
  • Payments work end to end but against test credentials, not a live account
  • Sign-in is a development shortcut — real phone-number login is a later phase
  • Riders and ops have no app; both are later phases
  • Shops have WhatsApp and a minimal screen, not the full dashboard
  • Tax invoices and shop payouts are the next phase
What happens next

The next milestone is five real orders.

The build is far enough along that the useful next step is not more code — it is one friendly kirana, a handful of real households, and five orders carried end to end. That is what turns every number in the model above from an assumption into a measurement.

What we need to get there: a live payment account, one pilot shop, and the delivery and payout phases finished.

Prove the basket

Does the repeat-basket feature actually get used? Target: half of all orders assisted by it by month six.

Prove the shop

Can a real kirana owner run a day of orders on WhatsApp alone, with no training and no hand-holding?

Prove the margin

Does a real basket land near ₹600, and does batching two deliveries onto one trip work in a real neighbourhood?
